A leaderboard attack-success number reads as a property of the model. It is a property of the contract. YHZ measures its five owners: admission rule, information contract, session wrapper, compute allowance, execution evidence. We move one at a time under pre-registration, every other byte frozen, so each number belongs to an owner. Cryptography is the setting, not the claim. On matched tasks one sentence decides. State the zero-reference premise and nearly every Claude- and GPT-family session recovers the plaintext on turn one; withhold it and success collapses, one-way in all sixteen paired cohorts (fifteen Holm-significant, FWER 0.05). Our own undeclared rule moves the same fixed programs 32 points; an output-preserving cleanup lands at +3.9 against the declared -0.8, so the movement was the rule's. The wrapper is a null: advantage bounded at 10.7% (Wilson 95%). Outside our harness, three published graders lift correct programs from zero to 18 of 282. A published protocol at its declared 12,000-token allowance leaves 30 of 60 sessions with no action, 10 provider-filtered, so 20 of 50 solve; raising it gives 28 of 50 and 8 of 10 pairs, a 16-point swing the budget owns. Seventeen slots never returned output: unmeasured, not failures. None of this grades an agent; it prices the contract. An out-of-domain audit-forensics task reproduces the premise effect (8/8 vs 0/8), five preconditions (the YHZ floor) complete the standard, and the share shrinks as models strengthen: the method travels, the magnitude does not. Frozen cohorts and audit trail are public.
